# English Language — Learn the Rules (Understand + Remember version)

> Phase 1 Paper 1 English = **20 questions, ~15 min**. Most scoring section. Target: 16+.
> **Real exam split (2024 + Jan-2026 papers):** RC 5–7 · Error spotting 4–5 · Double fillers 4–5 · Sentence improvement 3–4 · Para jumbles 4–5.

## How to use this file
Every rule below has three parts:
- **WHY** — the plain-English logic, so the rule makes sense instead of being memorised blindly
- **REMEMBER** — a one-line hook/mnemonic
- **Example** — ✗ wrong vs ✓ right

Read it through 2–3 times. After that, each time you get a question wrong, come back to the rule it broke — that's how the rules permanently move into your head.

# PART 1 — THE GRAMMAR RULEBOOK (understand version)

## AGREEMENT RULES (verb matches subject)

**Rule 1 — Verb follows the SUBJECT, not the nearest word.**
- WHY: Grammar only sees the subject. In "the list of items", the thing on the desk is the *list* — "of items" is just decoration describing the list.
- REMEMBER: **"Cross out the middle."** When you see `subject + of/along with + words + verb`, mentally delete the middle and check.
- ✗ The list of items **are** on the desk → ✓ The list of items **is** on the desk.

**Rule 2 — each / every / one of / either / neither → SINGULAR.**
- WHY: "Each" literally means "every single ONE". You're talking about one item at a time, even if there are hundreds.
- REMEMBER: **"Each ONE is single."**
- ✓ Each of the boys **has** a pen. ✓ One of my friends **is** a doctor.

**Rule 3 — "a number of" = plural · "the number of" = singular.**
- WHY: "A number of students" is just a fancy way of saying "many students" → plural. But "the number of students" means the *count itself* — a number is one thing → singular.
- REMEMBER: **A number = many people. THE number = one digit.**
- ✓ A number of students **were** absent. ✓ The number of students **is** rising.

**Rule 4 — "along with / as well as / together with" → verb follows the FIRST subject only.**
- WHY: These phrases are side-comments, not real additions. "The captain, along with his men, was rescued" — the sentence is still about the captain; "his men" is extra info between commas.
- REMEMBER: **The tagalong doesn't count.**
- ✓ The captain, along with his men, **was** rescued.

**Rule 5 — Collective nouns (team, jury, committee, board) → SINGULAR.**
- WHY: When the group acts as one unit (the team *wins*, the board *decides*), it's one thing. Only split to plural when members act separately — SEBI almost never tests that case.
- REMEMBER: **One team = one thing.**
- ✓ The jury **has** given its verdict.

## NOUN RULES

**Rule 6 — Uncountable nouns stay singular, never take "-s" or "a".**
- WHY: You can't count them — there's no such thing as "one furniture, two furnitures". Furniture/information/advice/luggage/news are lumps, not units.
- REMEMBER: **Can't count it? Can't add "s".** (Use "a piece of advice", "items of furniture".)
- ✗ The furnitures **are** new → ✓ The furniture **is** new. ✗ an advice → ✓ a piece of advice.

**Rule 7 — Some nouns are ALWAYS plural (take plural verbs).**
- WHY: They're born plural — scissors have two blades, trousers two legs, premises is a set of buildings, police/cattle/people are groups by definition.
- REMEMBER: **"Police in trousers with scissors entered the premises and counted the people's cattle."** (One silly sentence holds the whole group.)
- ✓ The police **have** arrested him.

**Rule 8 — "The + adjective" = a plural group of people.**
- WHY: "The poor" is short for "poor *people*" — many people hidden inside one word.
- REMEMBER: **The poor = poor PEOPLE.**
- ✓ The rich **get** richer.

## TENSE RULES

**Rule 9 — since = a POINT in time · for = a LENGTH of time → both take perfect tense.**
- WHY: "Since" answers "*since when?*" → a starting dot (Monday, 2019, childhood). "For" answers "*for how long?*" → a stretch (5 years). Both describe something that started in the past and continues → present perfect (*has worked*).
- REMEMBER: **SINCE = Starting point. FOR = Full length.** (S→S, F→F.)
- ✓ He **has worked** here since 2019 / for five years. ✗ He works here since 2019.

**Rule 10 — yesterday / ago / last week / in 2020 → SIMPLE past only. Never "has/have".**
- WHY: The perfect tense is for unfinished/unspecified time. The moment you name a finished time, you force simple past.
- REMEMBER: **Named the year? Kill "has".**
- ✗ He **has left** yesterday → ✓ He **left** yesterday.

**Rule 11 — Two past actions: the EARLIER one wears "had".**
- WHY: Past perfect ("had + V3") exists only to show which action came first when two past events meet.
- REMEMBER: **In a past story, "had" = it happened before that.**
- ✓ When I reached, the train **had left**. (Train left first.)
- Special fixed pairs: **No sooner …than** / **Hardly, Scarcely …when**: ✓ No sooner had he left **than** it rained. (Never mix: "no sooner when" is wrong — pair them correctly.)

## STRUCTURE RULES

**Rule 12 — Parallel structure: everything in a list wears the same uniform.**
- WHY: A list joined by "and/or/but" is a team — members must be the same form (all -ing, or all base verbs, or all nouns). Mixing = grammatical foul.
- REMEMBER: **Same list = same costume.**
- ✗ He likes reading, writing and **to swim** → ✓ …and **swimming**.

**Rule 13 — Correlative partners come in fixed pairs.**
- WHY: These are married couples — each first-half has ONE legal partner: either→or, neither→nor, not only→but also, both→and, whether→or.
- REMEMBER: **Neither brings NOR. Not only brings BUT ALSO.** Mixing pairs is the #1 trap.
- ✗ Not only he is smart **and also** kind → ✓ …**but also** kind.
- Bonus: with either…or / neither…nor, the verb follows the NEARER subject: Neither the boys nor the teacher **is** here.

**Rule 14 — Articles: "an" goes by SOUND, not letter.**
- WHY: The article exists to make speech flow — it's the *sound* that matters, not the spelling.
- REMEMBER: **Say it aloud — "university" = "yoo-niversity" → starts with a consonant SOUND → "a university". "Hour" = "our" → vowel sound → "an hour".**
- ✓ a European, a one-rupee note, an MBA, an honest man.
- "the" = specific/unique/second-mention (the RBI, the sun, the boy *I told you about*).

## COMPARISON RULES

**Rule 15 — "than" needs a comparative; never double up.**
- WHY: "More" and "-er" are two ways of doing the same job — using both is like saying "more better-er".
- REMEMBER: **One comparison per word.** taller than ✓ · more taller ✗.
- ✗ He is **more faster** → ✓ He is **faster**.

**Rule 16 — senior / junior / superior / inferior / prefer → take "to", not "than".**
- WHY: These come from Latin where the comparison is built in — they don't need "than" at all; English pairs them with "to".
- REMEMBER: **The senior citizens and the superior officers all go TO the party — never THAN.**
- ✓ She is senior **to** me. ✓ I prefer tea **to** coffee.

## PREPOSITION RULES

**Rule 17 — Fixed prepositions are married pairs — memorise, no logic.**
- WHY: Collocations are habit, not rules — "different from" is correct simply because English speakers agreed on it.
- REMEMBER (chunk them): **differ-FROM · comply-WITH · consist-OF · insist-ON · depend-ON · good-AT · interested-IN · capable-OF · accused-OF · suffer-FROM · married-TO · listen-TO · look forward to + V-ing.**
- ✗ different **than** → ✓ different **from**.

**Rule 18 — Some verbs already have the preposition inside — adding one is wrong.**
- WHY: "Discuss" = "talk **about**". "Enter" = "go **into**". "Reach" = "arrive **at**". The preposition is built into the verb's meaning — adding it doubles it.
- REMEMBER: **The word swallowed its preposition:** discuss(about) ✗ · enter(into a room) ✗ · reach(at) ✗ · comprise(of) ✗ · order(for food) ✗.
- ✗ We discussed **about** the plan → ✓ We discussed the plan.
- (Careful: "enter into an agreement/contract" IS correct — a real phrase. The ban is only for physical entering.)

## CONNECTOR RULES

**Rule 19 — "Unless" already means "if…NOT" — never add another negative.**
- WHY: Two negatives cancel. "Unless you do not work hard" literally means "if you work hard" — the opposite of what you meant.
- REMEMBER: **Unless = if-NOT. The "not" is built in.**
- ✗ Unless you **do not** work → ✓ Unless you work.

**Rule 20 — One bridge per gap: although…yet ✗, because…so ✗, since…therefore ✗.**
- WHY: Each connector does the whole job alone. "Although" already sets up contrast — adding "yet/but" puts two doors in one doorway.
- REMEMBER: **One sentence = one connector.** Keep either side, drop the other.
- ✗ Although he is rich, **yet** he is sad → ✓ Although he is rich, he is sad. (Or: He is rich, yet sad.)

## PRONOUN & MODIFIER RULES

**Rule 21 — After a preposition, use the OBJECT form (me/him/whom/us).**
- WHY: Prepositions only hold objects — "between you and me", "to whom". "I" and "who" are only for doers.
- REMEMBER: **Test trick — split it: "between me" ✓ sounds right, "between I" ✗ sounds wrong. Your ear already knows.**
- ✗ between you and **I** → ✓ between you and **me**. ✗ **Who** did you give it to? → ✓ **Whom** did you give it to?

**Rule 22 — Dangling modifier: the doer stands right after the comma.**
- WHY: An opening "-ing/having" phrase describes whoever comes next. "Walking down the road, a snake bit him" claims *the snake* was walking.
- REMEMBER: **After the comma → the actor.** If the actor can't do the action, the sentence is broken.
- ✗ Walking down the road, **a snake bit him** → ✓ Walking down the road, **he saw a snake**.

**Rule 23 — Pronouns agree with their owner.**
- WHY: "Everyone/each/someone" is one person → singular pronoun (his or her).
- ✓ Everyone should do **his or her** best.
- Reflexive (myself/himself) needs the same person doing the action: ✗ He **myself** did it → ✓ He **himself** did it.

## MEANING-TRAP RULES

**Rule 24 — Redundancy: words that repeat their own meaning.**
- WHY: "Return" = go **back**. "Repeat" = do **again**. The second word is the meaning of the first — saying both = saying it twice.
- REMEMBER (the dirty dozen): **return back · repeat again · revert back · refer back · recall back · free gift · past history · future plans · advance planning · true fact · end result · both alike.**
- ✗ Please return **back** my book → ✓ Please return my book.

**Rule 25 — Adjective vs adverb: linking verbs take adjectives.**
- WHY: "He drives *carefully*" — carefully describes the driving (verb → adverb). But "the soup tastes *good*" — good describes the SOUP, not the tasting. Verbs like is/seem/feel/look/taste/smell/sound just *link* to a description of the subject.
- REMEMBER: **"Tastes good" describes the soup, not the taste-buds. Soup isn't performing an action → adjective.**
- ✗ The food tastes **well** → ✓ The food tastes **good**.
- ✗ He drives **careful** → ✓ He drives **carefully**.

**Rule 26 — Conditionals: the more imaginary, the more past.**
- WHY: English pushes tense backward as reality fades:
  - Real possibility → If it **rains**, I **will** stay. (present → will)
  - Imaginary now → If I **were** you… (past form for present fantasy — always *were*, never "was")
  - Impossible past → If he **had studied**, he **would have passed**. (past perfect → would have)
- REMEMBER: **Each step away from real = one step back in tense. "If I were…" — wishes always take *were*.**

**Rule 27 — hardly/scarcely/few/little already carry a negative — don't double it.**
- WHY: "Hardly" = almost NOT. "Doesn't hardly" = not-almost-not = meaning flips.
- REMEMBER: **Hardly IS the "not" — ✗ doesn't hardly → ✓ hardly works.**
- Few vs a few: **few = almost none** (negative vibe), **a few = some** (positive). ✓ Few officers passed = almost nobody passed. Same for little/a little.

**Rule 28 — "It is high time" + PAST tense.**
- WHY: The phrase means "this should have happened already" — the action is overdue, so English dresses it in past form.
- REMEMBER: **High time = overdue = past.**
- ✓ It is high time we **went** home.

**Rule 29 — "had better / would rather" + bare verb.**
- WHY: They're fixed modal-like phrases that take the verb raw, no "to", no "-ing".
- ✓ You **had better leave** now. ✓ I **would rather stay**.

# PART 2 — THE 5 QUESTION TYPES (with mini walkthroughs)

## Type 1 — Error Spotting (4–5 Qs)
Sentence split into (A)(B)(C)(D), pick the wrong part, or (E) No error. Variant: pick the one correct sentence of four.
**Method — run the checklist in this order:**
1. Find subject → agreement (Rules 1–5)
2. Spot time words (since/for/yesterday) → tense (9–11)
3. Spot a list → parallelism (12–13)
4. Spot trap words → than/to, preposition, unless, although (15–20)
5. Nothing? "No error" is a real answer (~1 in 5).

## Type 2 — Double Fillers (4–5 Qs)
One sentence, two blanks, word-pair options.
**Method — grammar first, meaning second:**
1. Kill options by verb-form/plural grammar on blank 1 (usually halves the options).
2. Same on blank 2.
3. Still tied? Read tone — "although" → opposites, "and/because" → same direction.
4. BOTH words must fit — a pair with one perfect + one iffy is a trap.
**Why this works:** SEBI writes options so 2–3 pairs die on pure grammar before meaning is even tested.

## Type 3 — Sentence Improvement (3–4 Qs)
Underlined part + 5 replacements (one = "no improvement").
**Method:** it's error spotting on the underlined part only — same checklist. Check the WHOLE sentence with the swap in, not just the swapped words. "No improvement" is right ~1 in 5 — don't force a fix.

## Type 4 — Para Jumbles (4 Qs)
5–6 scrambled sentences, find the order (or "which is 2nd/3rd").
**The pairing method — remember "OPC":**
- **O — Opener:** the sentence needing nothing before it. Kill sentences starting with he/this/they (pronouns need an earlier noun), but/however/also/thus (connectors need a previous idea), the + noun already introduced.
- **P — Pairs (the real engine):** hunt links —
  - pronoun → its noun ("the move" → the sentence introducing the move)
  - "a X" before "the X" (indefinite introduces, definite refers back)
  - name first → surname/pronoun later
  - question → answer · problem → solution · general → example · date order
- **C — Check:** read your chain once. A wrong order *feels* broken mid-way — trust that bump.
Closer = conclusion words (thus, finally, overall).

## Type 5 — Reading Comprehension (5–7 Qs)
~300–400 word passage + detail/inference/vocab/tone questions.
**Method:**
1. **90-sec skim** — first + last sentence of each para only. You now have the map.
2. Vocab-in-context questions first (easiest marks): re-read the exact sentence, slot each option in — pick what keeps the meaning (NOT the dictionary meaning).
3. Detail Qs: scan back for the keyword. Answer ONLY from the passage — outside knowledge is a trap.
4. Inference Qs: one logical step beyond the text, not two.
5. Kill options with **always/never/completely/entirely** unless the passage literally says it.
6. Tone: SEBI passages are usually **analytical/cautionary**. Praise words → optimistic; flaw words → critical; just facts → neutral.
**6-minute hard cap. If a question needs re-reading the whole passage → guess, flag, move.**

# PART 2B — WORKED EXAMPLES (every question type, solved the way you'd solve it in the exam)

## Type 1 — Error Spotting (solve in 25–35 sec each)

**Ex 1.** *"One of my friend (A)/ has gone (B)/ to Delhi (C)/ for a conference. (D)/ No error (E)"*
→ **(A) — "friends."** Rule 2: one of + PLURAL noun + singular verb. *Trap: "has gone" looks fine, so people hunt B.*

**Ex 2.** *"He as well as his brothers (A)/ were present (B)/ at the meeting. (C)/ No error (D)"*
→ **(B) — "was present."** Rule 4: tagalong doesn't count — verb follows "He" only.

**Ex 3.** *"I have been (A)/ working here (B)/ since five years. (C)/ No error (D)"*
→ **(C) — "for five years."** Rule 9: since = point, for = length. Five years is a stretch.

**Ex 4.** *"The teacher asked the students (A)/ that why they (B)/ were late. (C)/ No error (D)"*
→ **(B) — drop "that."** Indirect questions take no extra conjunction: "asked them why they were late." *SEBI's favourite double-connector trap.*

**Ex 5.** *"Scarcely he had (A)/ reached the station (B)/ when the train left. (C)/ No error (D)"*
→ **(A) — "Scarcely had he."** Negative openers (scarcely/hardly/no sooner/never) flip subject-verb — inversion, like a question.

## Type 2 — Double Fillers (30–40 sec each)

**Ex 1.** *"The company decided to ____ its workforce after the merger led to ____ losses."*
(a) expand/minimal (b) **trim/heavy** (c) hire/expected (d) retain/negligible
→ **(b).** Meaning first: merger + losses → cutting, not growing → kills (a)(c)(d) on direction alone.

**Ex 2.** *"____ the evidence was strong, the court acquitted him as guilt was not proved ____ reasonable doubt."*
(a) Since/within (b) **Although/beyond** (c) Because/above (d) Unless/in
→ **(b).** Contrast opener → "although"; "beyond reasonable doubt" is a legal COLLOCATION — the vocab bank just earned a mark.

**Ex 3.** *"The RBI's move is expected to ____ inflation and ____ investor confidence."*
(a) raise/dent (b) fuel/erode (c) **curb/boost** (d) worsen/raise
→ **(c).** Both verbs are collocation pairs from the bank (curb inflation, boost confidence). (a) and (d) make one blank right each — the classic one-perfect-one-iffy trap.

**Ex 4.** *"He is so ____ that even his closest friends ____ him."*
(a) generous/admire (b) **arrogant/avoid** (c) kind/respect (d) humble/praise
→ **(b).** "so…that even" sets a negative extreme — only (b) has both blanks going the same negative direction.

## Type 3 — Sentence Improvement (25–35 sec each)

**Ex 1.** *"He denied <u>to help</u> me."* → (a) for helping (b) **helping** (c) to be helping (d) no improvement
→ **(b) "denied helping."** Deny takes the -ing form; refuse takes "to". Know which verb owns which costume.

**Ex 2.** *"I am living in Mumbai <u>since</u> 2019."* → (a) from (b) **have been living** (c) lived (d) no improvement
→ The swap must fix the VERB too, not just the preposition → **(b) "I have been living in Mumbai since 2019."** *Trap: changing only "since→for" is still wrong — the tense is the real error.*

**Ex 3.** *"No sooner did the bell ring than the students rushed out."* → (a) when (b) then (c) before (d) **no improvement**
→ **(d).** Already correct — "no sooner…than" is the legal pair. "No improvement" is right ~1 in 5 — checking it saves you from forcing fake fixes.

## Type 4 — Para Jumbles (one full walkthrough)

*"Arrange: (A) Such trust is what keeps money flowing into markets. (B) SEBI was set up in 1988 to protect investors' interests. (C) Since then, its role has expanded to cover intermediaries and new products. (D) Without such oversight, investors would hesitate to part with their savings. (E) It received statutory powers in 1992."*

**Solve with OPC:**
- **O:** Only B introduces SEBI by name — E's "It", C's "its", D's "such oversight", A's "Such trust" all need something before → **B first**.
- **P:** B(1988) → E("It" = SEBI, and 1992 continues the date chain) → C("Since then" = since 1992) → D("such oversight" = C's expanded role) → A("Such trust" = the confidence D's investors gain).
- **C:** B→E→C→D→A reads clean — intro, legal status, growth, why it matters, payoff.
→ **Order: B-E-C-D-A.** *The marks are in the links (It→SEBI, Since then→1992, such oversight→expanded role, Such trust→confidence) — never order by "feel".*

## Type 5 — RC (mini-passage + the 4 question species)

*"Algorithmic trading now executes most orders on Indian exchanges in microseconds. It has narrowed spreads and deepened liquidity. Yet regulators worry the same speed can amplify crashes when machines sell simultaneously. SEBI's response has been measured — kill-switch norms and circuit filters rather than outright bans."*

1. **Vocab-in-context:** "amplify" here means → **magnify/intensify** (not "cause" — the crash exists, algo makes it bigger). *Slot each option into the sentence and keep the one that survives.*
2. **Tone:** → **analytical/cautionary** — lists benefits AND concerns, ends with a balanced regulator. (SEBI passages are almost always this tone, never "hysterical".)
3. **Inference:** → **SEBI prefers safeguards over prohibition** — one step beyond "measured response", not two. ("SEBI wants to ban algos" = two steps too far.)
4. **Detail kill-option:** *"Algo trading has been banned in India"* → false — passage says the opposite. *Options that flip or exceed the text are the trap design.*

- **Phrases:** at large · in vain · on behalf of · in lieu of · at stake · in the wake of · on the verge of · in tandem with · in compliance with

# PART 4 — EXAM-DAY GAME PLAN

**Order:** fillers (4 min) → error spotting (5) → improvement (3) → jumbles (4) → RC last (6, vocab Qs first).
**Rules of thumb:** "No error"/"No improvement" is right ~20% of the time — don't force one. Fillers: both words must earn their place. RC: passage only, never outside knowledge. −0.25 marking → 50/50 guesses worth it, blind ones not.

**Daily drill:** 10 min — 5 rules from Part 1, rewritten from memory with your own example. 15 min — 20 mixed MCQs (Testbook chapter tests, real weightage). 5 min — log every wrong answer with the rule number it broke.
